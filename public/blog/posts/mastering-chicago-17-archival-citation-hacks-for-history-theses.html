What is the Chicago 17 approach to archival collections?

The Chicago Manual of Style (CMOS) treats archival materials with a specific hierarchy. Unlike a published book, which has a title, author, and publisher, an archival document relies on its location and identification numbers. In a Chicago style footnote guide, the fundamental rule is to move from the specific to the general.

Typically, your footnote should follow this logical order:

  • Title or description of the item (if no formal title exists, provide a descriptive one).
  • Date of the document.
  • Name of the collection.
  • Name of the series (if applicable).
  • Name of the repository/archive.
  • Location of the repository (e.g., city, country).
  • Identification number (e.g., box number, folder number).

How to handle missing information in primary source citation rules

Archival documents are notoriously incomplete. You might encounter an undated memo or a manuscript without a clear author. When these gaps occur, don’t panic. Chicago 17 provides clear pathways for handling missing data, ensuring your primary source citation rules remain consistent.

If a document has no title, create a brief, descriptive one and enclose it in quotation marks. Do not use italics. If the author is unknown, simply omit the name and start with the title or description.

Handling dates is equally critical. If the document is undated, use the abbreviation "n.d." in place of the year. If you can approximate the date based on context, use "ca." (circa) followed by the year in square brackets. Remember that precision is your greatest ally; when in doubt, be as descriptive as possible so that future researchers can retrace your steps in the archive.

Mastering archival document references in your bibliography

While footnotes are the "where" of your research, your bibliography is the "what." In archival document references, you don't necessarily need to list every single letter or memo you cited in your footnotes. Instead, you should list the collection by its name and the institution that houses it.

Follow this structure for your bibliography entry:

  1. Name of the Collection. Name of the Repository. City, Country.

This entry serves as a signpost, telling the reader which archives you utilised. It helps the reader understand the scope of your primary research without cluttering the bibliography with dozens of individual folder references.
