Common Chicago 17 Citation Traps to Avoid

Even seasoned researchers fall victim to the specific demands of Chicago style. By identifying these common pitfalls early, you can streamline your writing process and ensure your manuscript meets university standards.

  • The Ibid. Trap: In the 17th edition, Chicago discourages the use of 'ibid.' for consecutive citations of the same source. Instead, use a shortened citation format. Overusing 'ibid.' is a frequent error that examiners flag during review.
  • Inconsistent Shortened Citations: Once you have provided a full citation, subsequent mentions should use the author’s surname and a shortened title. If you are inconsistent with the degree of shortening, you disrupt the readability of your footnotes.
  • Incorrect Formatting of Multi-Author Works: For three or more authors, Chicago 17 requires the use of 'et al.' in the notes, but all authors must be listed in the bibliography. Failing to differentiate between these two sections is a common troubleshooting bibliography errors issue.
  • Hyperlink Clutter: While digital sources are standard, ensure your URLs are clean. Avoid including long, messy tracking parameters that detract from your academic presentation.

What is the Correct Order for Chicago 17 Bibliography Entries?

The bibliography acts as the definitive list of your research. Chicago 17 mandates that entries be alphabetized by the author's last name. If a work has no author, use the title. Crucially, the bibliography should be formatted with hanging indents to ensure the reader can scan surnames easily.

If you find that your reference list is generating errors, it is often due to missing metadata in your citation software. Always double-check that your 'Date Accessed' fields for online sources are consistent, as Chicago 17 has specific preferences regarding when an access date is required versus when it is optional.

How to Handle Complex Multi-Source Footnotes

Often, a single footnote will contain multiple sources to support a complex argument. Chicago style requires these sources to be separated by semicolons. Keeping these ordered logically—usually by the relevance of the source to your argument—is an art form that elevates your scholarship.

Avoid the temptation to use footnotes for substantive 'extra' commentary unless absolutely necessary. Excessive use of long, descriptive footnotes can distract from your main argument. If the information is critical, it should be in the body text; if it is peripheral, it might not be needed at all.
