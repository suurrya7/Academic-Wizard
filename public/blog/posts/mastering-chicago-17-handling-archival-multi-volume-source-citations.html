What is the Chicago 17 approach to archival documentation?

The Chicago Manual of Style (CMOS) 17th edition treats archival sources differently than published books. Because archives are often unique and unpublished, the priority is clear identification—ensuring your reader can locate the exact folder and item you consulted. When dealing with multi-volume sets, clarity regarding the specific volume number is non-negotiable.

Whether you are using digital databases or visiting a physical reading room, you must provide the name of the collection, the repository, and the location. How to structure footnotes for multi-volume archives

In Chicago style, footnotes follow a hierarchical structure. You move from the specific item to the general collection. When you encounter a multi-volume archival series, you should treat the volume number as an integral part of the identification process.

Consider this standard format for a footnote:

  • Author of document (if applicable).
  • Title or description of the item.
  • Date of the item.
  • Series name and volume number.
  • Name of the collection.
  • Name of the repository (archive).
  • Location of the repository.

Managing complex source referencing in your bibliography

While footnotes require specific item details, your Chicago style bibliography formatting for archival sources is slightly different. You typically list the collection rather than every individual letter or memo. If a multi-volume collection is central to your thesis, you should cite it as an entry under the collection title.

However, if you have utilised specific volumes extensively, it is acceptable to specify them in your bibliography or a 'List of Archives' section. Consistency is your primary goal. Never leave a reader guessing which volume of a ten-volume ledger set provided your primary data point.

Best practices for dissertation citation management

Dissertation citation management is not merely about mechanical adherence to rules. It is about transparency. When you document multi-volume archival sources, ask yourself: "If I were a researcher reading this in five years, could I find this document based on my own citation?"

Here are three key tips for keeping your references accurate:

  1. Create a master log: Document the full repository details the moment you view an archival record. Never rely on your memory to fill in the volume or series number later.
  2. Verify volume vs. box: Archives often use "box" numbers alongside "volume" numbers. Chicago 17 requires specific labelling—make sure you aren't conflating the two.
  3. Use shorthand for subsequent citations: After your first full note, use a shortened title or the collection abbreviation for subsequent references to improve the readability of your thesis pages.

Common pitfalls to avoid

One common error is treating archival volumes like published book volumes. Published books usually have a publisher and place of publication; archival volumes do not. Another frequent mistake is omitting the archive's location in the bibliography.
