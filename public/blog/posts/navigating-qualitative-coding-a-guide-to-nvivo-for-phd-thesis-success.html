How to Structure Your Coding Framework

Before you dive into the software, you must have a clear strategy. Coding interview transcripts for research should never be an ad-hoc process. You need a structured approach to maintain academic rigour.

  • First-Cycle Coding: Begin with descriptive or "in-vivo" coding to capture the essence of what participants said.
  • Second-Cycle Coding: Move towards identifying patterns, which leads naturally into thematic analysis in NVivo.
  • Node Hierarchies: Create a clear parent-child node structure. This allows you to drill down from broad themes to granular, evidence-based details.

A Step-by-Step Guide to the NVivo 15 Workflow

Follow this workflow to move from raw transcripts to a finalised thematic map:

  1. Data Importation: Import your Word or PDF transcripts into the 'Files' folder in NVivo. Ensure they are correctly formatted with headings if you plan to use 'Auto Code'.
  2. Familiarisation: Read through your transcripts without coding. Write memos in the 'Memos' folder to capture initial thoughts—this is vital for qualitative reflexivity.
  3. Initial Coding: Highlight key phrases in your transcripts and drag-and-drop them into new 'Nodes'. Use descriptive labels.
  4. Refinement: Review your nodes. Are some too similar? Merge them. Are some too broad? Split them into sub-nodes.
  5. Thematic Synthesis: Move from descriptive nodes to analytical themes. This is where your unique contribution to knowledge emerges.

Remember that qualitative analysis is iterative. You will likely return to your transcripts multiple times throughout your research. Common Pitfalls in Qualitative Methodology

New researchers often fall into the trap of "coding everything." Remember that qualitative research methodology tips suggest that coding should be purposeful. Not every sentence in an interview is a "finding." Focus on the data that directly answers your research questions.

Additionally, avoid the temptation to let AI do your thinking. While AI tools exist to assist, the intellectual labour of interpreting participants' lived experiences must remain yours to maintain ethical research standards.
